Abstract

We asked which pages a content editor should review first, and how much of a model's apparent skill at ranking them survives an honest audit. We used an anonymized snapshot of 30,000 pages from 32 clients (trailing 90 days), with the 78.8-million-row FlyRank warehouse release used to write and check the data contract on one March 2026 partition. We built a transparent rule baseline and a Random Forest against a rule-defined proxy label, validated by holding out whole clients, then audited leakage with ablations and a deliberately planted leak. Out-of-fold AUC across clients was 0.946 against a 0.42 base rate, but fell to 0.658 once four columns that define the label were removed, and pages flagged by the rule for a click-through gap showed a decline-proxy rate of 0.614 against 0.542 overall. The output is a ranked, reason-coded review queue for human editors: decision-support, not a forecast of what a refresh will do.

1. Introduction and problem statement

A content team that manages tens of thousands of pages can only open a few dozen a week. The decision is therefore not “is this page declining?” but “which pages do we open first, and what do we check when we do?” A wrong call costs differently in each direction. A page ranked high that needs nothing burns scarce editor time and erodes trust in the list. A page that needs attention but never surfaces keeps losing position quietly, because nobody was told to look.

That asymmetry makes this a ranking problem, judged at the top of the list. We therefore measure precision@K and AUC rather than overall accuracy, and we always report the base rate beside them.

The question this paper answers has two parts. First: can a transparent rule and a learned model produce a useful review queue from observable search metrics? Second, and more important: when the model scores well, is it finding something new, or re-reading the rule that defined its label? The second part turned out to matter more than the first.

2. Data

Modelling snapshot

The model, baseline and playbook use an anonymized starter snapshot: 30,000 pages × 44 columns, 32 clients, one row per page, with trailing-90-day search and engagement metrics, content metadata and freshness fields. Every row has at least one impression in the 90-day window, and the minimum content age is 90 days. Pages and clients carry pseudonymous hashed identifiers; the snapshot contains no client names, domains, URLs, page titles or search queries.

Warehouse release

The FlyRank warehouse release (build v20260703, gated) holds 78,835,655 daily rows of page-level performance from 27 January 2025 to 30 June 2026, plus a client table of 104 clients and a content table of 519,606 items. We used it for one purpose: to write and verify the data contract on a mid-panel month. The March 2026 partition has 9,841,378 daily rows from 55 clients, which aggregate to 331,437 page-month rows with no duplicate (client, page) pairs. The final month (June 2026) was kept sealed and never touched. No model in this paper was trained on the full warehouse.

Why this distinction matters. The headline model numbers describe the 30,000-page snapshot. The warehouse is where the next, stronger study belongs (Section 5), because it supports a past-to-future label that a single snapshot cannot.

What we excluded, and why

  • trend_direction, trend_pct, is_declining_label are never features. The decline label is computed from them, so using them would be circular.
  • Pseudonymous identifiers are used for grouping and splitting only, never as features.
  • Position zero means “no data”, not rank zero (1,205 pages, 4.0%). These are routed to a data check rather than scored on position.
  • The warehouse’s sample table covers the sealed final month, so it was not used for any label logic.
  • Analytics flags can be true, false or null. In the March partition only 4.2% of row-days were confirmed available, so we filter on the flag and never read zeros as “no engagement”.

Only aggregates and charts appear on this page. No row-level data, identifiers or queries are published.

3. Methodology

Label and assumptions

No editor has confirmed which pages needed review, so the target is a proxy defined by a rule. A page is “high priority” if it is either a page-one decay case (average position 1–10 and impressions down more than 20% against the prior 30 days) or a stale but visible case (over 60 days since update and over 50 impressions in 30 days). This marks 12,694 of 30,000 pages as high (base rate 0.423). Because the label is a rule over ordinary columns, scores measure agreement with the rule, not with an editor.

Baseline

The baseline is a readable score with hand-set constants and no fitting. A page is visible at 50 or more impressions in 30 days, stale at 90 or more days since update, and has a CTR gap when its position is within 20 and its CTR is below 0.35%. The score adds a 0.6-weighted CTR-gap term and a 0.4-weighted staleness term, each scaled by log impressions. Each page gets one reason code. Before using either signal we tested it:

Staleness was mixed: the decline-proxy rate was 0.511 for pages updated within 30 days and 0.611 for 91–180 days, but the oldest bucket fell back to 0.471 because pages that go truly stale mostly lose their traffic too. The rule therefore uses staleness only when a page is still visible.

Features and models

The models use 28 features (20 numeric, 8 categorical): search and keyword metadata, 90-day impression, click, session and AI-referral volumes (log-scaled), CTR, engagement, position and freshness fields, and content type. Missing keyword and word-count data get explicit indicator flags instead of zero-fill. We fit a logistic regression first (every coefficient inspectable), then a Random Forest, because the label is an AND/OR combination of thresholds that a linear model cannot represent directly. All seeds are 42.

Validation design

Splits are grouped by client: whole clients are in training or in test, never both, which approximates scoring a client the model has never seen. We report three views: one 75/25 client split (24 training clients, 8 test clients), random 5-fold as the naive contrast, and client-grouped 5-fold across all 32 clients with out-of-fold scoring. A time-aware split was not possible because the snapshot is a single trailing-90-day export with no dated rows.

Leakage checks

  1. Banned-column guard. A check asserts that none of the three label-derived columns is in the feature set.
  2. Planted-leak test. We deliberately add trend_pct to confirm the harness would catch a real leak.
  3. Ingredient audit. We list which features are direct ingredients of the label, which overlap its window, and which come from an existing decision system (none did).
  4. Ablation. We retrain without the suspect columns and compare.
  5. Permutation importance on a held-out fold, to see what the model actually leans on.

A small earlier demonstration on the warehouse partition showed the size of the problem: honest accuracy on a toy decline label was 0.711, and adding one label-derived column lifted it to 0.999.

4.3 What the model was leaning on

The four most important features by permutation importance were freshness_tier, position_tier, days_since_last_update and avg_position. All four are direct ingredients of the label definition. Removing them is the test that matters:

This is circularity rather than future information: in deployment those four columns would be known at scoring time. The consequence is the same either way. A high AUC here largely reflects agreement with a rule, and should not be read as evidence that a refresh will help.

4.4 Where the model without the ingredients is wrong

Using the model without the ingredient and volume columns, and setting K to the number of true highs (12,694) so that errors are balanced by construction, precision in the top K is 0.535 against a 0.423 base rate: a modest, directional lift. About 64% of the missed pages are high only through page-one decay, which depends on the decline signal that is banned from the features. Recall is 70.3% for stale-but-visible pages and 32.4% for decline-only pages. This is a structural gap and not a bug to patch: adding the decline columns would push AUC toward 1.0 and mean nothing.

5. Limitations and honest framing

What this work cannot claim is as important as what it shows. Each limit below is measured or stated in the notebooks.

  • The target is a proxy. No editor-confirmed outcome exists. Scores measure agreement with a rule.
  • The strongest features are ingredients of the proxy. Read the 0.946 beside the 0.658. The model is a second opinion, not the basis of the ranking.
  • One snapshot, no time split. Nothing here says how the ranking behaves on next month’s pages. The warehouse supports a past-to-future design with a sealed final month; that is the study worth running next.
  • No causal claim about refreshing. The data compares different pages at one moment, not the same pages before and after. Who gets refreshed is also a choice someone made, so part of any gap is the choosing. We cannot say that a refresh reverses decline or how many days a page can wait.
  • Population filter. Every page had at least one impression in 90 days, and 38% have fewer than 50 in the last 30. Low-visibility pages are out of scope for a refresh action, which does not mean they are fine.
  • The queue is dominated by large clients. The largest single client holds 50% of the global top 50 and 52% of the top 250.
  • Uneven client coverage. Ten of 32 clients have under 200 pages, so the model cannot be verified there; two verified clients fall below 0.80 AUC.
  • The CTR benchmark is crude. It is the weighted CTR by position tier within this slice, and the top-3 tier is small.
  • Effort and cost figures are assumptions, not measurements, and are not reported as results.

We are not claiming to have predicted any search engine’s behaviour. The claim is narrower: in this portfolio, these observable signals produce a ranking that agrees with a defined proxy, and part of that agreement is circular.

6. Ranked recommendations

These are decision-support for human review, ordered by how well the evidence supports them.

  1. Work the CTR-gap lane first.It holds 8,780 pages and is the only reason code where the independent decline proxy sits above the catalogue (0.614 against 0.542). Before editing a title, check the results page: an answer box or AI overview can absorb clicks no rewrite will recover.
  2. Review in small, frequent batches.Take the first 50 pages as tier one and the next 200 as tier two. The first 250 hold 29% of the recoverable-clicks ceiling, and returns fall steeply after that.
  3. Treat “stale but visible” as a question, not a verdict.Ask whether the last update was a bulk or template change rather than an editor reading the page, and whether the information is actually out of date. The staleness signal was mixed in every test.
  4. Work the queue per client.Each client’s team should use its own client-level rank, or a client-balanced view for a mixed sitting, because the global top is dominated by the largest clients.
  5. Use the model only as a second opinion.Let reason codes carry the explanation. Where the model disagrees with the rule (26% of the queue) or the client is weak or unverified, judge the page on its reason code, and ignore the model on the ten under-covered clients.
  6. Fix the data before the page.For the 1,205 pages with no position, pull it from Search Console first. Require a second reviewer for any page with 50,000 or more impressions in 30 days, since very high-volume rows can be tracking glitches, and for every prune-review page.
  7. Build the feedback loop that makes the next study possible.Log each accept, reject or defer with a one-line reason, and hold about 10% of tier one and two back as an untouched comparison group. Without it, whether actioned pages improve cannot be measured.

Never automate or claim: auto-publishing or rewriting content from the queue; auto-pruning, deleting or redirecting pages; promising clients a traffic uplift; presenting a score as a probability of success; adding the decline columns as features to “fix” the blind spot; or retraining on the same proxy and reporting a higher AUC as progress. Monitoring should watch editor acceptance, input drift on CTR and position, the share of missing positions, and any shift of more than ten points in the action mix; the thresholds are proposals for FlyRank to agree, not findings.
